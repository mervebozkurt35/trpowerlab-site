Daily price spread

The daily spread is the gap between the most and the least expensive hour of the day-ahead price. It is a first estimate of what a battery could earn from buying in the cheapest hour and selling in the most expensive one, before efficiency losses, fees and the limits of the battery. The second line, the standard deviation, measures how far hourly prices move around the day’s average. Both are monthly averages of daily values. Euros (the default) make years comparable; lira values rise with inflation. The percentage view divides by the month’s average PTF, so it shows spread relative to the price level: a spread of 100% means the gap between the cheapest and the dearest hour equals the average price.

The cost of being out of balance

When the system is short, the operator buys extra energy in the balancing market and SMF usually rises above PTF. When it is long, SMF usually falls below PTF. The gap is what a market participant risks by being out of balance in that direction: imbalance prices are built from PTF and SMF. Bars show the monthly average gap, split by system direction, in euros, lira or as a percentage of the month’s average PTF. The percentage is the best way to compare years: a gap of 300 TL/MWh is a large share of a 150 TL/MWh average price and a small share of a 2,000 TL/MWh one.

The highest short-side gap in percentage terms is May 2026: SMF was 1,528 TL/MWh above PTF while the month’s average PTF was only 591 TL/MWh, so the gap equals 259% of the average price. The lira gap itself was also well above the 290 TL/MWh median of the previous twelve months: this suggests that balancing prices did not fall with the low day-ahead prices. Percentages exaggerate gaps in months with a low average PTF, so read them next to the lira and euro views.

Intraday versus day-ahead

The intraday market (GİP) trades the same hours after the day-ahead auction. Its average price (AOF) minus PTF shows whether buying later was cheaper or dearer on average. A gap close to zero means the two markets agree; persistent gaps point to forecasts that changed after the auction, for example for wind and solar.

Hours at zero

PTF falls to zero when supply that cannot or will not reduce output, such as run-of-river hydro, wind, solar and must-run plants, exceeds demand. Zero-price hours are a sign of surplus and of the value of storage and flexible demand.
